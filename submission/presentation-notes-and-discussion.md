# Design Methods in Practice - Assignment notes

Major Studio · MS Data Viz

Reading: Amy J. Ko, [Design Methods, 2nd edition](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/books/design-methods/)

## Summary notes - maximum 5 minutes

Target: 4:45, with a 15-second buffer.

### 0:00-0:40 | Design is a way of reasoning [1-2]

Amy J. Ko's Design Methods presents design as a practice of generating, communicating, and evaluating ideas. It reaches beyond visual appearance. The book introduces methods for understanding a situation, exploring possibilities, and learning from what happens. I am applying those methods to DVC's first career panel. Our goal today is to practice design reasoning; we do not need to finish an event plan.

### 0:40-1:25 | Understand people before choosing a solution [3-4]

A career panel is already a proposed solution. First, we need to identify whose situation we want to improve. Someone comparing careers may need different support from someone seeking a first internship. Interviews and observation can help us understand those experiences. We then synthesize what we learn into goals, personas, scenarios, and a clear problem argument. These representations need grounding in research. Our classroom vote will select a hypothesis to investigate, rather than establish what all attendees need.

### 1:25-2:05 | Generate alternatives and make them visible [5]

Creativity involves practice, context, and a willingness to explore. We can ask people for ideas, borrow from other settings, question assumptions, and externalize possibilities. For DVC, we might compare shared questions across speakers, individual pathway stories, and anonymous Q&A. Each format could support a different need and introduce a different tradeoff. Making alternatives visible gives us something specific to compare before committing to our first idea.

### 2:05-2:55 | Prototype to answer a question [6-7]

A prototype should help us learn something that affects a decision. We choose its detail around that uncertainty. Our question form explores whether someone can contribute without speaking to the room and understand what happened afterward. Typing and submitting provide input; draft and submitted are states; the confirmation provides feedback. This is relevant to interactive visualization: a filter or selection also changes state, and the display must help people interpret the result.

### 2:55-4:00 | Distinguish three ways of evaluating [8-10]

Critique examines reasoning through dialogue: someone explains a choice, and others question its goals, assumptions, and consequences. Empirical evaluation observes people using a design. Analytical evaluation inspects a design using principles or a structured walkthrough to predict difficulties. These methods offer different kinds of evidence. A participant completing our form can reveal something about that interaction, but it cannot establish whether the career panel provides useful advice. That broader claim needs investigation in a relevant setting with the intended attendees.

### 4:00-4:45 | Iteration and the studio connection [2]

The relationships between these methods matter more than treating chapters as a checklist. Feedback may change an interface, suggest another format, or make us redefine the problem. Participation, values, and power also shape whose ideas count. For our visualization practice, the takeaway is to connect audience, purpose, representation, interaction, and evidence. We will now make three decisions and keep track of the assumptions we still need to investigate.

## Three discussion questions - 15 minutes

After the summary, use three five-minute blocks. The app and its G guide retain the original compact 15-minute route; this outline provides the assignment’s 5 + 15-minute structure.

### 01. Whose problem are we solving, and what evidence might change our mind?

Time: 5:00-10:00 | Chapters 2-4 | App tabs: The case + The problem

Name a specific group of intended attendees. Choose a working need: comparing careers, finding a first step, asking candid questions, or another need. Discuss a concrete experience we would need to learn about from those people.

Facilitate: 0:30 name the group; 1:00 choose a hypothesis; 2:30 discuss evidence and missing perspectives; 1:00 record the reasoning.

Optional probes: Ask what finding would challenge the choice, who is absent from this classroom, and whether an interview, observation, or another source would help.

Record: One audience, one need, and one piece of evidence to seek. Keep disagreement visible.

### 02. Which panel format fits that need, and what tradeoff are we willing to accept?

Time: 10:00-15:00 | Chapters 5-8 | App tabs: Alternatives + A prototype + Critique

Give pairs a minute to generate possibilities before using the three on-screen options. Select a provisional format and connect it to the need. Try the question form as one possible touchpoint, even if anonymous Q&A is not the selected format.

Facilitate: 1:00 generate; 1:30 compare; 1:30 volunteer task; 1:00 critique and record.

Optional probes: Discuss whom the format might leave out, what the small prototype can teach us, and one revision supported by a reason.

Record: One format, its rationale, one tradeoff, and one proposed revision.

### 03. What would convince us that the design helps attendees, beyond a usable question form?

Time: 15:00-20:00 | Chapters 8-10 | App tabs: Evaluation + Reflection

Use the evaluation choice question to distinguish critique, observation, and inspection. Then propose evidence for the attendee need chosen earlier. A smooth form interaction and a useful career event are different claims.

Facilitate: 0:30 concept check; 2:30 discuss evidence; 1:30 propose a follow-up study; 0:30 name a transfer to visualization.

Optional probes: Specify the participants, task or setting, and an observation that would make us revise the design. Discuss what one classroom volunteer cannot establish.

Record: One evaluation plan and one method to carry into a visualization project.
